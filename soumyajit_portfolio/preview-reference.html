The story

It started on a sofa in Kolkata.

One conversation taught me that objections are often clues — not the whole story.

2017. A customer said no twice.

I was trying to sell a study plan to a father who had already turned me down. I stopped pitching and asked what he was really worried about. It wasn't the money. It was whether the plan would still matter years later.

We built a long-term plan around both children's needs. That conversation shaped how I approach selling to this day.

“The objection people say out loud is rarely the real one.”
My operating belief

Diagnose before you persuade.

Customer trust, commercial clarity and an honest understanding of the problem are more valuable than a rehearsed pitch. I learned to sell by listening, then carried that lesson into partnerships, team coaching and revenue ownership.

Problem solving

Problems I solved — in the real world.

Behind every metric is a commercial problem, a messy conversation or a process that needed to change. These cases show how I approach the work.

The case narratives below are deliberately more detailed. Documented outcomes are identified as such; scenarios marked illustrative are examples of the kind of problem-solving approach, not claims of a specific completed incident.

TOPPR · CUSTOMER TRUST · DOCUMENTED STORY

Turning a repeated “no” into a conversation about the family's real need.

Problem: A father had already declined the education plan more than once. Continuing to push the same features, discounts or urgency would likely make the conversation less productive. The challenge was to understand whether the objection was price, trust, timing or a genuine mismatch.

Diagnosis: I moved away from the immediate purchase and asked about the family's education priorities and timeline. The concern was long-term usefulness for both children—not simply the monthly cost or the product in front of him.

Approach: I reframed the discussion around a longer horizon: what the younger child would need through school and how the elder child's board-exam timeline affected the decision. Instead of treating the product as a one-time sale, I explained the plan as a family-level education decision and made the value relevant to their situation.

Reported outcomeThe conversation led to a long-term education plan of approximately ₹2.5 lakh, as captured in the portfolio story.

What this taught me: objections are signals. The seller's job is to uncover the real decision criteria before attempting to persuade.

OYO · PARTNER ECONOMICS · DOCUMENTED

Getting hotel owners to evaluate the partnership as a business—not a brand pitch.

Problem: Hotel owners were making a commercial decision about their property. Brand visibility alone wasn't enough; they needed to understand the revenue model, trade-offs, operating expectations and whether the partnership made sense for their business.

Diagnosis: The owner’s perspective was the property’s economics: occupancy, revenue-share terms, operational expectations and confidence in the partner. A generic sales pitch could not address those concerns.

Approach: I worked through owner conversations and commercial negotiations, explaining the partnership and negotiating revenue-share terms directly. I focused on the business case and on making the onboarding conversation concrete, rather than relying on the brand name to do the selling.

Reported outcomeOnboarded 500+ rooms across West Bengal and Andaman & Nicobar Islands.

What this taught me: partnership sales is a two-sided business case. The agreement has to work for the company and for the partner.

VEDANTU · B2B PARTNERSHIPS · DOCUMENTED

Building school partnerships where the buying decision is shared across stakeholders.

Problem: Selling into a school isn't the same as selling to one individual. Leadership, teachers and administrators can have different priorities, and a promising conversation may not progress unless the value is specific to the institution.

Diagnosis: The sales challenge was to identify what mattered to each school, understand who influenced the decision and move the discussion from a broad introduction toward a clear next step.

Approach: I opened and developed B2B conversations with schools, built relationships with institutional stakeholders and maintained follow-up around the school's needs. I also trained BDAs, extending the work beyond my individual selling activity.

Reported outcomeSigned approximately 20 school partnerships and generated reported revenue of ₹40–50 lakh. Revenue period is not specified, so this figure is not labeled monthly or annual.

What this taught me: institutional selling needs stakeholder mapping, a tailored value proposition and deliberate follow-through—not a single presentation.

SCALER · INDIVIDUAL PERFORMANCE · DOCUMENTED

Improving the conversion conversation by treating discovery as a skill, not a script.

Problem: High-intent leads can still fail to convert when discovery is shallow, the underlying motivation is missed, or the advisor jumps into the pitch too early. More calls don't automatically solve a conversion problem.

Diagnosis: I looked at the quality of the discovery conversation: whether the prospect's goal, constraints, urgency, decision process and fit were understood; and whether the next step was earned through the call rather than pushed.

Approach: I worked on my own call discipline, questioned the gaps in discovery, and used performance consistency as a feedback loop. As I moved into leadership, I carried the same lens into call coaching and seller development—focus on the behavior and skill that moves the funnel.

Reported outcomeAs an individual contributor, generated approximately ₹30–40 lakh in monthly revenue. Following promotion to Associate Manager, delivered approximately ₹75–80 lakh in monthly revenue. The earlier portfolio also records 8–9 monthly closures against a 4–5 target for three consecutive quarters.

What this taught me: conversion improvement starts with understanding where the conversation breaks down. Activity is an input; quality and progression are the work.

ACCREDIAN · HIGH-TICKET CONSULTING · ILLUSTRATIVE

Separating genuine career intent from curiosity before recommending a premium program.

Problem scenario: A professional may be attracted to a high-value upskilling program but still be unclear about the target role, time commitment, financial comfort or realistic outcome. A product-heavy pitch can create interest without creating a sound buying decision.

Approach: Start with the current role and experience, then clarify the desired transition, why it matters now, the candidate's constraints and what they have already tried. Make the gap explicit. Only then map relevant program components and discuss the investment in context.

Sales discipline: If the desired outcome, effort or fit is unclear, don't manufacture urgency. Resolve the gap—or disqualify responsibly.

What this demonstrates: the ability to structure complex, high-consideration sales conversations without defaulting to features or pressure.

INTERVIEW KICKSTART · SALES ADVISORY · ILLUSTRATIVE

Turning a broad career ambition into a specific problem the advisor can actually help solve.

Problem scenario: A prospect says they want a better job, higher compensation or a move into AI—but that goal alone does not reveal what is blocking progress. The gap could be technical capability, interview readiness, positioning, experience articulation or job-search execution.

Approach: Break the goal down: current experience, target role, timeline, strengths, gaps and constraints. Reflect the diagnosis back to the prospect to check understanding. Then map only the program elements that address the gap, clearly distinguish what the program can support from what the learner must do themselves, and agree on a realistic next step.

Sales discipline: Make the recommendation specific and defensible. Don't promise a job outcome or imply that enrolling alone solves the problem.

Outcome statusIllustrative advisory case. It is not presented as a verified learner incident, result or placement claim.

What this demonstrates: structured discovery, expectation setting and consultative recommendation—skills that scale trust and reduce mis-selling.
